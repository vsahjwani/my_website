SoCalGuessr: Street-Level City Classification

What stuck with me about this project is that two street photos from different Southern California cities can look almost identical to a person, yet the model I built tells them apart correctly 80% of the time. The patterns that separate one city from another are there in the data, we just aren't built to see them the way a model can. To get there, I engineered 255 vision features from 9,100+ street-level images, using HOG, LBP, and Gabor filters to capture texture and edge information, then reduced that feature space with PCA and kept the strongest signals with ANOVA. On top of those features, I trained a regularized feedforward neural network in PyTorch, using BatchNorm, Dropout, Adam, and early stopping to keep it from overfitting, for the final 6-way city classification.

Seafood & Health: Predictive Modeling

kareenf04.github.io/seafood_health_analysis

Through this project I learnt that good feature engineering can lead to the simplest and most accurate models. The question behind this project was whether seafood was distinctly healthy. After a little bit of research, creating nutritional blend feature of high protein, relatively low calories, and a unsaturated fat led use to the answer. I quantified that blend as a composite health score across 83,000 recipes, and once that feature existed, the rest of the problem became much simpler. A Decision Tree, tuned with GridSearchCV, used that health score to predict whether a recipe contained seafood with 92.1% test accuracy.

Rent the Runway: Fit Prediction

github.com/vsahjwani/cse-158

This was a four-person team project, and we coordinated the whole thing remotely, across different cities and schedules. What made it work was treating the collaboration itself as seriously as the modeling: we kept the codebase clean and reproducible, checked in with each other frequently, pushed continuous updates instead of one big handoff at the end, and ran peer evaluations to hold everyone to the same standard. On the technical side, I combined TF-IDF text features from 192,000 user reviews with engineered numerical features like BMI and body ratios, then trained a Gradient Boosting ensemble, tuning it with RandomizedSearchCV and benchmarking it against random forest baselines. The final model reached 77.06% accuracy, 9 points above the prior published state of the art.

TriageIQ In Progress

While a lot of ML projects use a model to predict what happens next, this one uses ML to look backward instead. Using interpretable models, we can understand where real-world patterns support what we'd expect and where they go against it. I built a pipeline using XGBoost, SHAP for explainability, and TF-IDF on chief-complaint text, trained on 300,000+ CDC emergency room records to model patient acuity and wait time. A reviewer raised a point that reframed the whole project for me: predicting a triage outcome doesn't actually help a clinician in the moment, it just hands them another number to interpret under the same time pressure. So instead of using the model to prescribe decisions, I'm now using the same data to inspect them, looking at where acuity and wait time actually line up, where they don't, and what that gap reveals about how triage decisions get made in practice.
