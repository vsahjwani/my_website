Shots of Mumbai in 2008

How Media Coverage and Representation of the 2008 Mumbai Attacks Shaped Perceptions and Memories of this Event












175 deaths, 10 attackers, and over 62 hours of live media coverage. In this essay I explore how media coverage and cultural representation of the 2008 Mumbai attacks shaped perceptions and memories of both this event and global terrorism as a whole, while also reinforcing stereotypical narratives. Using the feature film Hotel Mumbai directed by Anthony Maras and Terror in Mumbai (2009) directed by Dan Reed and Ashwin Neal Mani, alongside academic analyses of the attacks and their media coverage, I examine the cultural significance of creative choices and the retelling of stories to appeal to certain audiences. Hotel Mumbai displays the dramatised depiction of the 26/11 attacks, highlighting human pain and individual bravery while distilling the 62-hour siege into a narrowly contained story focused on the Taj Hotel. Whereas the 2009 Channel 4 documentary Terror in Mumbai reconstructs the assaults using real news video, victim testimonies, and intercepted phone conversations between militants and their handlers, portraying the incident as both an immediate spectacle and a forensic record.

On 26th November 2008, ten Pakistani men reached Mumbai by boat, infiltrating the city through the fishermen’s slums. They targeted South Mumbai’s Chhatrapati Shivaji railway station, the popular Leopold Café, two hospitals, and a theatre using hand grenades and bullets. They continued their attacks at 3 locations - the Nariman House, where a Jewish outreach center was located, the Oberoi Trident Hotel and The Taj Mahal Palace & Tower - where they also held hostages. The terrorists were in constant contact with their lead operator in Pakistan who instructed them through their extremist acts.

The intensive media coverage of 26th November through 29th November included TV channels showcasing live broadcasts, premature news reports, and 7 feature films, 2 web series, 4 documentaries, 8 nonfiction books, 5 other books, and a plethora of articles and reports about this event. These media outlets have caused panic, amplified the trauma, and displayed the tragedies in warped proportions promoting misinformation. 

While the movie Hotel Mumbai is efficient as a thriller and successfully employs immersive filmmaking techniques, it depicts an inaccurate representation of events, emphasises certain characters disproportionately, and exaggerates the emotional narratives, diminishing the graveness of the attacks by theatricising it to appeal to vaster audiences. Morgenstern proposes the question “when we're besieged by reports of mass shootings and images of anguishing slaughter, how do we justify watching more of the same as entertainment unless it tells us something we didn't know?” (Morgenstern, 2019).

The violence lasted for four days and affected at least six locations throughout Mumbai. Unmentioned in the film, it diminishes the siege to less than twenty-four hours and focuses only on the shootings at the Taj hotel. The severity of the attacks, which claimed 166 civilian lives throughout the city, is lost due to the compression of time and space. Human beings also struggle to truly comprehend large numbers - whether 150 or 200 people died, the emotional impact registers much the same. Thus, while the film briefly mentions the statistics, its decision to dramatize only a handful of deaths and a few survivors minimizes the true scale of the debacle. Even if Arjun and Chef Hemant Oberoi’s roles perfectly convey the courage of hotel workers, by focusing on foreign guests - characters most viewers outside India could easily identify with - the film ensures that the heroes in service survive, maintaining a sense of emotional peace for the audience. However, many more than two employees played integral roles in saving lives, and eleven of them died doing so. That omission softens the discomfort of confronting survivor’s guilt, but it also silences the sacrifice of those who bore the heaviest cost.

Furthermore, the film generally ignores the opinions of Indian survivors in favor of foreign visitors by giving them the most nuanced and relatable depictions. Indian stories of locals at work, enjoying a meal at one of the Taj’s well reputed restaurants, or about to be married at the Taj itself, with all their loved ones to celebrate with them lack the relatability of the fictional Zahra - David plot on a global scale. This decision, which filters an Indian tragedy via Westernized empathy lenses, exposes a lack of cultural sensitivity. The movie also attempts, in a dubious way, to humanize the terrorist Imran by showing his hesitation and youthful gullibility. Such an approach is more appropriate for a larger investigation of radicalization than for a recounting of a single tragedy, where it runs the risk of weakening accountability. According to Morgenstern, “The film makes a perfunctory and ill-advised attempt to humanize Imran; it's ill-advised not because he's less than human, but because the making of a terrorist is the subject of another movie with wider, and wiser, concerns than this one.” Considering the number of sacrifices made to factual accuracy for maintaining the engagement of the film, adding a layer of humanizing the terrorists seems counterproductive and disrespectful to those who were encountered with them.

In addition, the movie uses dramatic rather than realistic protracted episodes of horror and tense near-death scenes to heighten its cinematic effect by exaggerating emotional arcs. One striking example is when Arjun orders terrified guests in the dining hall to hide: “Don’t be scared. Stay quiet, stay calm. We’ll get through this” - a line that encapsulates the heightened emotional drama more than the chaotic reality. While engrossing the viewer, this method reduces the muddy truth of the attacks to a Hollywood movie with distinct heroes and villains. Even if this can be successful in arousing sympathy, it turns a national tragedy into a work of entertainment meant for a worldwide audience rather than a true cultural remembrance of what Mumbai went through.

All in all, movies like Hotel Mumbai highlight the important question of how far facts can be twisted to suit cinematic purposes, while also providing an immediacy of feeling that history texts cannot. Not only does historical trauma go misrepresented, but it also runs the risk of erasing voices, particularly local ones, that should be heard when an event is redefined to conform to audience expectations or narrative standards.

Although, this is not the case for the documentary Terror in Mumbai. Using primarily actual surveillance footage, the documentary focuses on realism to chronicle the terrorism. The direct use of Kasab’s hospital interrogation clips, actual locals sharing their experience, and how people working for the media risk it all to be at the attacks and end up intervening with the course of events.

One of the most striking elements of Terror in Mumbai is its use of Ajmal Kasab’s hospital interrogation clips and the attackers’ intercepted phone calls. Hearing his own words rather than an actor’s interpretation gives the impression of complete transparency: “They told us there was a job we must do,” “I was supposed to kill people there.” Yet, even this seemingly unfiltered footage raises questions. While Kasab’s shaky voice and youthful demeanor tap directly into pathos, humanizing him in a way that feels uncomfortable, we cannot know what was left out. The documentary seems brutally transparent, but its choices - what to show and what to omit - shape whether Kasab appears as a manipulated boy or a hardened terrorist.

Equally powerful are the stories of locals caught in the siege, some even showing their massive scars. These testimonies provide a rare glimpse into the personal scale of the tragedy, far beyond statistics. Stories include those of people who witnessed family members being shot, people who were police officers on call, people working near massacre scenes, describing the way the terrorists were dressed and behaved, people on their way to visit ill family members, and many more. Their stories aren’t relatable but intimate and devastating. Although, what happens to the stories of the survivors who aren’t comfortable to share their trauma in front of a camera pointing at them? The documentary offers immediacy, but its creative choices and logistical abilities inevitably curate which lives and which emotions are remembered.

A pseudo theme explored throughout the documentary is the role of the media. Real footage showed camera men shooting the terrorists driving in a police vehicle. “They drove towards a junction crowded with media people and both gunmen fired” said a police officer pretending to be killed in the back of the same vehicle. This clearly shows how the people behind a camera tend to pay less heed to the fact that they’re as present at any event as the victims or villains. One of the terrorists’ phone calls recorded, “The room is on fire. They're showing it on TV. I'm sitting in the bathroom” While the reason for him to be in the bathroom is not explicitly stated, it could be inferred to have some relation to the fact that if he was in the room he’d be on TV. And further displaying the power and desire of surveillance, the documentary uses the unnerving phone calls between the attackers and their controllers to narrate events of the film, throwing us viewers in the perspective of the attackers. The controller wanted to be on call to hear everything, sadistic in nature and maintaining his reign of power over the shootings while sitting in another country. Additionally, this entire documentary is also a showcase for the quote “Meaning is neither before nor after the act” (Svensson, 2012) displaying primary live footage from CCTV cameras along with clips of survivors telling their stories, helpless and emotional.

Thus, even while Terror in Mumbai is more immediate than Hotel Mumbai, the impressions it generates are nonetheless skewed by the biases and preconceptions of viewers as well as the framing decisions made by media producers. Because representations of history are created by biased, opinionated humans, any retelling - whether in the form of a movie, documentary, or news report - affects how history is remembered. A narrative belongs to the people who lived it; sharing it turns into a means of gaining control and making one's voice heard. In this way, Hotel Mumbai figuratively silences the locals: the movie favors non-Indian accents, and the only characters who speak Hindi are the terrorists. This distances the story from reality and commodifies Mumbai’s trauma for international consumption. Similar to the Atlas Group artworks, whose purposefully fictional archives are still interpreted as historical records, the issue is more acute with dramatized entertainment, as viewers frequently confuse fact and invention and internalize spectacle as reality. In this way, Hotel Mumbai does more than just fall short of expressing the city's communal anguish; it also rewrites the memory's ownership, alienating Mumbai from its own narrative.

The 2008 Mumbai attacks were not only an act of terror but also a media event that has since been retold, reframed, and reimagined through countless lenses. By emphasizing foreign viewpoints, streamlining chronologies, and exaggerating emotions in ways that misrepresent local voices and communal trauma, Hotel Mumbai turns the siege into a narrowly focused thriller. In contrast, Terror in Mumbai uses uncut videos, testimonials, and intercepted calls to provide the appearance of transparency, but it also selects which stories are published. These pieces serve as a reminder that media history is never objective; rather, it is influenced by audience demands, cultural bias, and selective storytelling. In the end, who keeps possession of memory depends on whose story is recounted and how. Hotel Mumbai runs the risk of overshadowing experienced reality, commodifying suffering for international consumption, and alienating the city from its own story.
