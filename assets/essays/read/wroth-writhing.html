Wroth Writhing

In Mary Wroth’s ‘Sonnet 77’ from Pamphilia to Amphilanthus, Wroth employs the labyrinth motif to convey her emotional paralysis and moral indecision while subverting traditional gendered roles. I believe that Wroth’s references to classical myth and subversion of Petrarchan norms remain relevant today for their insight into emotional self-governance, female subjectivity, and the enduring complexity of women’s roles in the 17th century.

Pamphilia to Amphilanthus is a sequence of sonnets written by Wroth in part of her larger prose romance, The Countess of Montgomery’s Urania, first published in 1621. It is divided into four distinct sections where Wroth navigates her feelings towards her unfaithful lover, William Herbert, sings her emotions, pleads forgiveness from Cupid for not trusting his guidance, and circles back to the darkness in the first section even though it hints at a hopeful outcome. 

The third section, a crown of 14 sonnets, opens with Sonnet 77 "In this strange labyrinth how shall I turn?” (l. 1) This opening line immediately introduces the central metaphor of emotional suffocation and inner confusion. The labyrinth becomes a map of Pamphilia’s psychological struggle with love, uncertainty, and self-doubt. In the lines “Thus let me take the right, or left-hand way, /Go forward, or stand still, or back retire;” (l. 9, 10) Wroth constructs Pamphilia as a woman negotiating her interior life, and the labyrinth becomes a metaphor for the tensions between desire and duty, autonomy and submission and eventual resolution by foreshadowing the end that loops us back to the maze. (Perry and Miller) This provides readers with a sense of entrapment within this cycle that reflects the experience of Pamphilia’s mental state. Wroth achieves the effects of mimetic writing as she plays on two key features of a labyrinth, enclosure and complexity (Moore). However, the audience is perplexed by the last line of this poem that discusses a solution “Is to leave all, and take the thread of love.” (l. 14) Many Elizabethan formal gardens had mazes or labyrinths (usually planted with box hedges) as forms of entertainment, like the maize maze at Penshurst Place, Wroth’s family home(“Amazing Sonnets by Lady Mary Wroth”). As Pamphilia considers following the thread of love, Wroth evokes a stark contrast: the intricate, thorny maze of shrubbery versus the delicate, uncertain thread. This image immerses readers in the risks and hopes tied to choosing love as a guide. This creates tension, almost frustrating the audience and effectively introducing the feeling of hopelessness.

This metaphor gains additional resonance through Wroth’s classical allusions, particularly the myth of Daedalus’s labyrinth. In Ovid’s version, the maze is a place of confinement and ritual violence, serving as a prison for the Minotaur, where youths are sacrificed to appease divine wrath. Theseus escapes this fate with Ariadne’s thread, a symbol of cunning and love, to navigate through the labyrinth and defeat the minotaur, but Pamphilia’s “thread of love” offers no such triumph. Instead of slaying the Minotaur, she surrenders to a more abstract threat: emotional paralysis, moral indecision, and the inscrutable will of Cupid. (Rumens)

Her vow, “yet that which most my troubled sense doth move / is to leave all, and take the thread of love” (ll. 13–14) becomes both a spiritual plea and a silent gesture of resignation. Farabaugh believes that Wroth “replaces the masculine, heroic logic of conquest with a feminized poetics of endurance and reflection” (Farabaugh, 2003), re-establishing the labyrinth not as a stage of heroism but as a psychological and social structure designed to suppress female agency. Thus, the maze's design becomes a metaphor for a world where love is a trap shaped by expectation, obeyance, and doubt. In early modern religious and cultural practice, sacrifice was a means of negotiating power with the divine. Pamphilia's poetic labor, offered as tribute to Cupid, functions in this tradition: a kind offering that also exposes the imbalance of power between poet and deity. Wroth flips traditional Petrarchan love poetry where the woman is silent, distant, and worshipped: Pamphilia speaks, but what she says is shaped by a world that demands modesty and submission. She becomes both the speaker and the sacrifice. “The currency of her poetry,” Gil writes, “is both constrained by and resistant to the systems of exchange that traditionally govern love and authorship” (Gil, 2001). Not only are Wroth’s classical allusions aesthetic choices, but they also serve as mirrors for Pamphilia’s internal struggle. Alluding to long-standing stories makes Wroth’s words more effective as she clearly sets up expectations of heroism, rescue, and escape, invoked from the classical myths. Since most readers of her writing period already had some understanding of the stories she references, her subversions are clear and impactful, drawing the audience further in to enter the maze with Pamphilia - not to watch her conquer it, but to feel the weight of speaking through structures designed to keep one silent and yearning for clarity. 

In Petrarchan convention, the beloved is voiceless, objectified by the poet’s gaze. Wroth subverts this dynamic as Pamphilia addresses Cupid instead of Amphilanthus, focusing the sonnet on her internal struggles. Almost every line of the poem contains a reference to herself using words like “I”, “my”, and “me” furthering engraving the focus on herself throughout the poem. Wroth illustrates the contradicting expectations of women in the lines “If to the right hand, there, in love I burn; / Let me go forward, therein danger is; / If to the left, suspicion hinders bliss, / Let me turn back, shame cries I ought return.”(l. 3-6) Here, a labyrinth can be interpreted as a convoluted social confinement, forcing Pamphilia to choose between her shame at turning backward or her fear of suspicion if she turns left, minding that to love too fervently is immodest and to doubt is morally suspect. These lines skillfully uncover the socially imposed walls that push the women of Wroth’s society into a social labyrinth.  The extensive discussion of each possible path on a separate line of the poem engrosses readers, producing an experience of Pamphilia’s dilemmas for them. 

Wroth’s uses linguistic play and a semantic field of agency to challenge Petrarchan norms of passive femininity. In the original manuscript of the sonnet, the first line, “In this strang labourinth how shall I turne?”(l. 1) exhibits an archaic spelling choice of the word labyrinth pointing directly to the labor-intensive vocabulary that populates this sonnet; Pamphilia is not simply lost in love but struggling to work through it without the typical labyrinth’s triumphant ending. In the line “Or help, but travail find for my best hire.” (l. 12), the word travail, often connoted childbirth, adds a gendered layer reflecting the double bind faced by women in love. To travel implied difficulty, exhaustion, and often danger especially for women, whose movement through both physical and social space was tightly regulated. Along with these instances of wordplay, the poet incorporates phrases like “hire,” “I ought return,”(l. 5) “in love I burn,”(l. 3) and “I must these doubts endure”(l. 11) that assert Pamphilia as a woman with agency. However, along with this agency come blame and effort which, unlike a classical labyrinth, don’t always end triumphantly or aren’t always escapable. Pamphilia is not rescued like Ariadne or triumphant like Theseus; she is instead the solitary traveler in a maze that offers no exit. This emphasis on labor allows Wroth to reclaim the Petrarchan tradition from passive femininity and infuses it with the active, self-aware struggle of a woman. Wroth’s puns demand close reading, encouraging readers to engage more deeply with the text. This attentiveness mirrors Pamphilia’s own careful self-navigation, making the sonnet both intellectually and emotionally immersive.

All in all, Mary Wroth skillfully opens her crown with ‘Sonnet 77’ where she skillfully applies the motif of the labyrinth, classical allusion, and Petrarchan subversion to dramatize the entrapment and endurance of love as both personal burdens and poetic labor. Wroth transforms the female lover from a silent object to a reflective subject, displaying the emotional complexities of women navigating conflicting expectations.
