Dulce et Utile

As seen in Aesop’s fable ‘The Lion & the Mouse’ and Dr. Seuss’ Horton Hears a Who! (1954), how do Aesop and Dr. Seuss integrate education and entertainment to create meaningful, evergreen literature?











The Latin word fabula comes from the root fari, to tell. (Lerer 36) Both Aesop and Seuss tell didactic stories by creating another world where their characters interact, showcasing life-lessons through entertainment. The stories, Aesop’s ‘The Goose & the Golden Egg’ and Dr. Seuss’ ‘Did I Ever Tell You How Lucky You Are?’, are apt in comparing how two legendary fabulists of different eras delivered similar morals in their own revolutionary evergreen fashion. In this essay, I will discuss how both Aesop’s and Seuss’ masterpieces not only educate them through the creation of characters that help turn abstractions into reality(Lerer 38) but also immerse children in phantom sceneries by employing structures, vocabulary and tone.

In both works, the artists effectively use anthropomorphism to mirror social situations, magnifying a certain moral value. In Aesop’s texts, he uses anthropomorphism to convey the persona of a character tied to an animal, therefore maintaining a concise writing style while Seuss’ use of anthropomorphism helps children see something deeply rooted in their minds with a completely new dimension. “Since animals were so deeply involved with their day-to-day physical life, it makes sense that the Greeks would incorporate them into their intellectual life as well.” Clayton explains the origins of Aesop’s tendency to turn to animals, emphasising his ability to reach and resonate with a large scope of people. Using phrases like “The Lion was much amused to think that a Mouse could ever help him.”, “huge paw angrily”, and “"Spare me!" begged the poor Mouse.” Aesop repeatedly relates a lion to qualities like arrogant, powerful, and feared in ‘The Lion and the Mouse’. This is an example of how Aesop does not pollute his didacticism with appointing qualities to human characters and instead referring to a certain animal, thus maintaining the simplicity of his texts. On the other hand, Dr. Seuss goes beyond Aesop’s personification of animals by illustratively anthropomorphs fantastical and exaggerated creatures, adding a more human layer of evolving characters, to educate children. In the text, “Horton Hears a Who”, Seuss efficiently exaggerates the effect of an elephant’s enormous ears by allowing it to hear a “Who” - which is as small as a speck of dust. This not only humours children due to the nonsensical nature of the talking elephant’s interaction with something so small but also goes on to subvert the idea of something small being insignificant through Horton’s determination to protect the speck for the single reason that “A person’s a person, no matter how small”. From the chapter ‘On Beyond Darwin’ in Lerer’s Children’s Literature, we can understand and apply a Darwinian perspective to analyse each of the author’s treatment of animal characters. Aesop’s process of building a character for an animal involves the theory of survival of the fittest. Depicting lions as king-like proud people with power and mice as timid, escaping, helpless creatures, Aesop is able to use Darwin’s theory of survival of the fittest to recruit the right animals by recognizing the inner character by looking at the outer form. Seuss however points to Darwin's theory of evolution to make his animals more human, according to the meaning of being human today, by allowing them to change their minds or learn through the course of the fable. An example of this can be seen in Seuss’ ‘The Lion and The Mouse’ through the contrast between the Kangaroos’ belief in life on the speck at the beginning of the fable versus the end. Due to the skillful use of anthropomorphism in both texts, children are seamlessly led through a journey of less biassed judgements towards the characters and events in the fable. Then again, children can easily observe reflections of the highly personified characters of fables in people they interact with and thus apply the morals from the corresponding stories with confidence of knowing that a positive outcome succeeds the corresponding course of action. Compared to the seemingly similar moral in “The Lion and The Mouse” that “A kindness is never wasted.”, Seuss’ kindness is based on the value of another “Who” and thinking about the other’s concerns, family, and future. In contrast, Aesop’s kindness attributes to the reciprocity of a kind deed, painting it as a possible profit after an investment. Thus, while didactic themes may be shared between Aesop and Seuss, the differences in them are further brought out through their distinct literary styles.

Although both, Aesop and Seuss, capture readers in their isolated worlds, their fables tremendously differ in style. Narrating each of their fables with different structures, vocabulary and tone, the writers lure children’s attention alike. Having lived most of his life as a slave, Aesop’s perspective was from an underprivileged point of view reflected in the transactional nature of the moral of this story along with common themes relating to greed, innovation due to necessity, etc. Lerer brings a pertinent argument to the table, “This is the language of the subordinate, and it is significant that fables were associated with the serving classes from earliest antiquity.” This explains the credibility of Aesop’s morals as we hear stories narrated by the defeated. Reading into the effects of being a slave, Aesop often must have received the shorter end of the stick. Therefore, his fables reflect values of fairness and collecting benefits for oneself and are delivered with a genuine tone by excluding the coating of verbose writing, elaborate vocabulary, and unnecessary repetitions. This intrigues readers, especially children, who seek the truth and clear conclusions, suitable to short attention spans. Seuss’ life was spent being the class clown, subverting expectations and making bold decisions to voice his opinions. However, Seuss’ fables are painted in love and laughter, rhyme and rhythm. “​​His creatures cannot be brought back by fishermen, but they can be drawn by human hands” (Lerer 185). Embellishing situations with linguistic garnishing to create a memorable experience for children, Seuss’ satire and wit tickle a reader’s mind and imagination. In ‘Horton Hears a Who’ the lines of rhyme and rhythm,

“Then Horton stopped splashing. He looked toward the sound.

‘That’s funny,’ thought Horton. ‘There’s no one around.’”

create a musical quality readers enjoy endlessly. Maintaining a playful tone, Seuss’s humorous and inventive language is evident when he describes the animals' disbelief, "Why, he looks like he’s hanging from the neck of a giraffe!" This intentional exaggeration and clever choice of vocabulary foster a light and engaging tone, even as Horton faces serious challenges.

Though both authors have their unique as well as shared factors, the most important commonality is their ability to educate and entertain children again and again even when they’re adults, for decades to come and since decades ago. “And  so  it  is  important  for  the  educator  to  appeal  to  children’s  taste,  but at the same time to control that taste: to limit childish urges and use  fables  for  both  positive  and  negative  example” (Lerer 37). Thus, Aesop and Seuss are both masters in storytelling as we can see in their ability to engage a reader’s imagination and intellect.
