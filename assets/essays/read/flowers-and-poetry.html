Flowers and Poetry

How does Carol Ann Duffy’s subversion of Little Red-Cap (1812) by Jacob Grimm and Wilhelm Grimm in her poem, “Little Red Cap” (1999) reflect the position of women in their societies. 













In the poem “Little Red-Cap,” Carol Ann Duffy subverts the classic fairytale Little Red-Cap (1812) by The Grimm Brothers through Little Red Riding Hood’s journey through maturity, effectively using the structure of a dramatic monologue. In this essay, I will compare the portrayal of women in each text, through a gynocritical lens and referring to the waves of feminism during which these works were produced. While both texts share similar gendered archetypes of the wolf or “wicked creature” and a “dear little girl”, they differ in the contexts in which they were written and thus, their interpretation by their audiences. 

Little Red Cap was “a dear little girl who was loved by everyone who looked at her.” Delving into the theme of sexualization of girls “At childhood’s end” (Duffy Line 1), while the Grimms maintained the patriarchal archetype of a “Sweet sixteen, never been, babe, waif,” (Duffy Line 12) Duffy subverts the narrative using the anaphora, “I first,”, “I knew”, “I crawled”, “I filled”, etc. Taking agency over the encounter, Duffy highlights the young protagonist’s agenda, “cajoled and captivated by the wolf’s poetry” (Burcu 568), directing attention to the wolf’s superior use of words, insinuating “male domination in art and literature, and greater publication opportunities men usually … have” (Burcu 568). Thus, using poetry as an allusion to flowers in the original tale, the wolf’s masculine archetype displays his clever use of words to seduce “babes”, luring them with their innocent desires, flowers and poetry. These archetypes demonstrate Judith Butler’s theory that gender is performative, which encourages readers to question whether the archetypes are gendered due to their given sex by the writers or due to our deeply rooted notions of gender roles that are reflected in these archetypes. 

“The central focus of this text is on the child's fascination with the wolf's wolfness (which may be read as a thinly veiled metaphor of masculine virility since the distinguishing wolf's features may also apply to mature men), which lures the child into the wolf's bed even though the child realizes that the figure in the bed could not possibly be her grandmother.” (Jones 102). Signaled with the colour red, the vicissitudes of a young girl's maturation are delved into as Jones mentions the explicit sexual innuendos that mustn’t be disregarded. “What little girl doesn’t dearly love a wolf?” (Duffy Line 22) Duffy rhetorically questions LRC’s fascination, implying more overtly the little girl’s attraction to the wolf was for reasons other than love, subverting the original innocence of not being able to tell the difference between the big, bad wolf and the little girl’s weak, old grandmother through which Duffy further employs feminist agenda to her protagonist. 

The Grimm Brothers’ tale was originally written by Charles Perrault, presenting his work to the literary salons of an aristocratic French audience. This emphasizes the Grimm Brothers’ choice of altering the text to end positively that reflects its presence during the first wave of feminism where the idea that inequal rights to women and men are what cause the difference in their spiritual and intellectual freedom. However, Duffy’s work produced during the first two phases of the third wave of feminism, identified with male oppression, faces a greater task of making giving her LRC the power, metaphorically through the axe she uses to murder the wolf. 

“I took an axe to the wolf

As he slept, one chop, scrotum to throat,” (Duffy Lines 38, 39) 

Scrotum could refer to men’s sexual domination over women while the throat refers to their verbal domination of women, in this case it might be specifically about women writers and artists having to censor themselves, while their male counterparts published their work without hesitation and dominated the literary world, based on Carol Ann Duffy’s position in the literary society, vis-à-vis her relationship with Adrian Henri. Through the shift in the ‘hero’ of the story, we can clearly see the shift from male gaze to female gaze where the female character isn’t portrayed as a damsel in distress anymore but instead from a

perspective that sees women as subjects instead of objects.

Thus, while the Grimms preserve Charles Perrault’s moral “Children, especially attractive, well bred young ladies, should never talk to strangers, for if they should do so, they may well provide dinner for a wolf.” Duffy’s moral is more empowering towards young readers, 

“Howls the same old song at the moon, year in, year out

Season after season, same rhyme, same reason.” (Duffy Lines 35, 36)

“The wolf, or the male genius considered superior throughout the ages, feeds on female support and imagination while either misrepresenting or completely disregarding these characters in his art.” (Burcu 568), therefore the solution is “The wolf has to be murdered, that is, the daunting presence of men has to be limited and their pressure on women in public and private spheres ended for women to reclaim their power that has been historically vilified as dangerous and demonic in line with the binary system establishing gender perceptions.” (Burcu 568). Further establishing Duffy’s ties to the radical third wave of feminism. However, both texts share a common moral that one cannot expect habits of the big, bad wolf to be improved, indicating the wolf’s power as well as hopelessness. 

Both texts end on a positive note, “Out of the forest I come with my flowers, singing, all alone” (Duffy Line 42) Furthering the connection between flowers and music (poetry) that represents the gain of physical and cerebral freedom, the success of the female voice after a long journey emphasized by the poem’s free-verse style and frequent use of enjambment. All in all, Duffy’s subversion of the classic preserves “The mixture of fear, revulsion, curiosity, and attraction that typify children's reactions to the enigma of sexuality” while subverting outdated gendered archetypes using the female standpoint.
