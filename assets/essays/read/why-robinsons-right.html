Why Robinson’s Right

Daniel Defoe’s Justification of Robinson Crusoe’s self-proclaimed power in The Life and Adventures of Robinson Crusoe (1719)











In the English Novel The Life and Adventures of Robinson Crusoe (1719), Daniel Defoe persuades his audience to believe Robinson Crusoe deserves his self-proclaimed power using a skillful combination of ethos and pathos. Defoe employs the colonizer’s perspective of Friday’s uncivilization, the adventurous tactics of Crusoe’s conquering of the island to convince readers of his hegemony, and the self-realization of Crusoe according to Christian beliefs, proving the novel to be a baton for colonial discourse.

Crusoe’s relationship with Friday commences as Crusoe saves the “poor creature’s life” (Defoe 160). The ethos of this scenario is grounded in values of reciprocity and gratitude leading Friday to be indebted to Crusoe for saving his life. “…taking me by the foot, set my foot upon his head; this, it seems, was in token of swearing to be my slave for ever.” (Defoe 161). Setting Crusoe’s foot above Friday’s head serves as a symbol of his devotion, however, it leads Crusoe to understand that Friday would be his slave indefinitely, featuring the socially accepted norms between the colonizer and the colonized through nonverbal communication, and deep-rooted psychological conditioning on the colonized mind which allows Friday to accept his fate. 

However, Lerer accentuates the importance of perspective in justification. Lerer questions, “But is there something of the cannibal in kings?” (Lerer 133). Did Crusoe kill a native to save Friday’s life or to take it? As we learn about Crusoe’s imposition of his language, lifestyle, and religious beliefs on Friday, we discover his truly dehumanizing practices as he attempts to wash away Friday’s past life. Justified by the contrast of Crusoe’s credibility as a white man able to survive alone with Friday’s uncivilized, “barbarous” cannibalistic tendencies imbibed from the “naked savages”, Crusoe takes on the White Man’s Burden (Kipling) to improve Friday as a person until he reaches Eurocentric standards. Even after Friday is objectified as an instrument to comfort Crusoe by speaking in his language, he remained servant and “still a Cannibal in his Nature” (Defoe 150), implying that no matter how much a native is taught to match Eurocentric standards, his unevolved species, distinguished by their large feet, would never attain them completely. 

Emphasizing the European theme of acquiring knowledge through adventure, Defoe documents Crusoe’s resourceful engineering endeavors where “details of craftsmanship stand out” (Lerer 136). This extends Crusoe’s credibility as a man of logic. Crusoe’s clever placement of his fort overlooking incoming sails further imply the interdependence of knowledge and power, thus justifying Crusoe’s power over the island and any life on it through his earned access to surveillance.

Crusoe’s ethos is further elevated through the novel’s didactic nature, leading a reader to believe that not only the book but also Crusoe is worth learning from. Thus, Crusoe’s relationship with Friday is justified by Crusoe saving Friday’s life literally and figuratively, by ‘civilizing’ Friday, which serves as an allegory to the process of colonization by European nations.

This epistolary novel gives us an insight into Crusoe’s repentance for his sins as he goes through the process of self-realization, augmenting the audience’s pathos for Crusoe. Playing on the shared beliefs of balance, the audience roots for Crusoe’s prosperity after facing hardships throughout his life. A new wave of luck serves as an analogy to the biblical story of the Prodigal Son. While Crusoe does not have faith in returning to his father when his missions are unsuccessful, he faces more misfortunes but as he learns to have faith in the Lord, he finds “perfect green barley, of the same kind as our European, nay, English barley.” (Defoe 61). Thus, Crusoe begins to not only survive, but gain power and conquer the island, crowning himself “my majesty the prince and lord of the whole island” (Defoe 177). This gain of power marks Crusoe’s new identity according to Denis Sindic’s theory “there is no identity without power”. Using his power, Crusoe births previously naked Friday by naming him, clothing him, and teaching him like a son “His very Affections were ty’d to me, like those of a Child to a Father”. In accordance with Christian beliefs, Crusoe serves as a compassionate Father figure to Friday reaching the other end of the biblical reference. Thus, Defoe creates pathos for Crusoe through his earlier hardships and character development, alongside his analogous journey to the Prodigal Son that all in all justify his right to colonizing tendencies that convenience him. 

In conclusion, this text serves as an apparatus to justify Western domination over colonized people. Through themes of indebtedness, Eurocentrically defined civilization, knowledge through exposure, and Puritan parallels, Crusoe gains credibility and sympathy from the audience which aid his justification in his capture of power over the island and the people who stumbled upon it. Thus, Defoe outlines the creation of the occident and orient for readers, impacting children’s impressionable perspectives.
